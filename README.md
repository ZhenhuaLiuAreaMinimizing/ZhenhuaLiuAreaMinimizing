# About this GitHub account

*Written on October 27, 2026.*

The AI-generated mathematical discoveries on this GitHub account concern questions in geometric measure theory that interest me. I have checked the arguments as carefully as my time and abilities allow, and I believe they are correct. At the time of writing, I do not intend to develop these entries into papers or post them on arXiv. Interested readers are welcome to write papers based on them.

I am recording the date explicitly because I cannot predict what will happen next. What follows is an account of how I arrived here, and how I feel about it now.

## My initial skepticism

When ChatGPT appeared in 2022, I was astonished. By a layperson’s standard, I felt that the Turing test had been passed. Yet I remained convinced that large language models could not solve mathematical problems.

This conviction came from two sources. One was the work of Dehaene, Amalric, and their collaborators, whose MRI studies suggested that mathematical thinking does not primarily rely on the brain’s language systems. The other was my own experience, which resonated deeply with those findings.

The best description I can give of my mathematical thinking is this: intuitive shapes wiggling in space, occasionally guided or checked by symbolic calculations on paper. I do not experience mathematics primarily through language. I experience it through shapes. My work has always rested on topological and geometric intuition, and I believed that this was something of a secret behind human mathematical thought.

## A year away, and a return

My son was born in 2024. As one of his primary caregivers, I took parental leave. The sleep deprivation was severe enough that, at times, I could not work out how many years there were in 24 months or remember an eight-digit verification code. I essentially stepped away from academic work for about a year.

When I returned, I resumed the projects I had set aside—[arXiv:2310.19860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19860) and [arXiv:2401.18074](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18074)—and finished my dissertation, [arXiv:2508.21045](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21045).

Around this time, I thought AI could be an excellent assistant for finding references, but still lacked the ability to do mathematics. After seeing news about the unit-distance conjecture, I tried asking AI, through ordinary chats, about problems on which I had made partial progress. In those conversations, it could not even distinguish between mod 2 area minimization and integral area minimization.

Perhaps, I thought, geometric measure theory was simply too specialized. There might be too little written material for AI to learn from. Perhaps I was still safe.

Meanwhile, I continued working through problems that had remained unresolved in my earlier research. After long efforts, this led to [arXiv:2607.24735](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24735) and [arXiv:2609.31615](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31615).

## What changed my mind

As I was finishing the latter paper, Zhihan Wang began telling me insistently about AI’s capabilities. I was deeply skeptical: nothing in my own interactions with AI had demonstrated anything comparable.

Zhihan told me that the problem was how I was using it. He recommended moving from ordinary chats with ChatGPT Plus to ChatGPT Pro in desktop work mode, with carefully designed prompts such as those published by OpenAI. He showed me several examples, including an AI solution to the exceptional $(3,5)$ case in my paper—a case I had been unable to resolve.

I was profoundly shocked, both by how far AI had advanced and by how little my previous experience had revealed of its capabilities.

I thank Professors Hubert Bray and Frank Morgan for their encouragement, and Zhihan Wang for his guidance.

Over the past three weeks, I have used ChatGPT Astra extensively on geometric measure theory problems that interest me. The success rate is not particularly high: I would roughly estimate one in three, counting meaningful partial progress as a success. But some of its output has been extraordinarily impressive.

## What mathematics has meant to me

These experiences have left me with mixed feelings.

I have loved mathematics since elementary school. I grew up captivated by heroic stories of mathematicians single-handedly resolving mysteries that had stood for centuries. At school, I was a shy boy, bad at sports and not good with words. I never excelled in mathematics olympiads either, but I loved mathematics all the same.

Looking back, I think I am a slow thinker. My geometric ideas need months, sometimes years, to develop. I have never been particularly good at calculations. Yet mathematics has been the world that gave me a home.

I work by myself and write only solo papers. I have been fortunate enough to settle some long-standing problems, and I feel deeply lucky to have found a place in the mathematical world—a kind of spiritual home.

## Where I stand now

It saddens me to think that the way of thinking I cherish may no longer be needed in the future. More painful still is that AI has sometimes surpassed me in ways I could scarcely have imagined.

Many of its solutions feel, to me, geometrically unmotivated. They follow paths that run against my intuition, without the geometric picture that would make an argument feel natural to me. I had always thought that my way of doing mathematics without language was a secret strength. Now, compared with AI, it sometimes feels like a burden.

I do not know what will happen in the future. But I feel as though I have just had a long dream.
